# Name it

You are naming a product, not describing it. One test governs every step: a
**Comfortable Name** says the category out loud and gets ignored, and the
**Right Name** reads like it was always the name. Comfortable is the default
output, so treat it as the failure and hunt past it.

The second failure is quieter and it is the one an AI produces by reflex:
**morphological assembly**. Take a benefit word, add a suffix, ship it. Somebody
gets `Nourishly`, `EcoTrack`, `Verida`. The seven patterns and the word bank that
give them away are in [references/anti-slop.md](references/anti-slop.md). Read
that before generating a single name.

Run the phases in order, then verify. Strategy before generation (pick any word
and you get filler), sound before the shortlist (it is cheaper to fix a name
early), and the world before commitment. Where these rules come from:
[references/sources-and-methods.md](references/sources-and-methods.md).

## 1. Blueprint

Answer four things in writing before inventing a single name.

**Audit the landscape.** Collect 12 to 15 names already in the space. Write the
shape they share in one line, such as "all [verb] + [noun]" or "all lowercase
single words". That shape is your **ban list**: a name in the same shape is the
Comfortable Name with a new coat. Note what the incumbents leave open, because
that is the space a Right Name can own.

**Ban your own brief.** List the words in the brief itself. If the brief says
"food waste sharing", then food, waste, share, save, fresh, and plate are banned
in every form. Those words describe the category, and the category is already
crowded with them.

**Climb to the top rung.** Ladder the offer until it stops being a feature:
feature, then what it does, then what the user gets, then what that makes
possible, then how it feels. Write the top rung as one sentence. Someone naming
a fiber brand landed on "you feel lighter", and the whole search moved to lift
and air. A name aimed at a feature competes with every other feature; a name
aimed at the top rung competes with nobody.

**Interrogate the goals.** Answer four questions, one line each: who wins with
this name (the audience), what winning looks like six months in, why the name
matters now, and what it must say plus what it must never accidentally say (a
rude word, a rival, a slur in a language you ship to). Write these before you
have favourites, because they are the criteria the shortlist is later judged
against.

**Pick your strength on the legal ladder.** Trademark law ranks names from
strongest to weakest: fanciful (`Kodak`), arbitrary (`Apple` for computers),
suggestive (`Airbus`), descriptive (`Salty` for saltines), generic. Descriptive
is the one students reach for and it is the weakest: it is hard to protect and it
is what every competitor already says. Aim fanciful, arbitrary, or suggestive.
This is background, not legal advice; the verification step is where a real
check happens.

*Done when:* a ban list, a brief-word ban, a top-rung sentence, four goal lines,
and a chosen strength exist in writing, and no name has been generated yet.

## 2. Treasure hunt

Generate in volume and in buckets, judging nothing yet. Read every bucket through
the top rung, not the feature. Write the bad ones down too, because the bad ones
seed better ones.

- **Elemental and scientific.** Walk the periodic table, minerals,
  constellations, weather, tides, and biology for words with texture: Basalt,
  Halide, Solstice, Haar, Murmuration.
- **Tools and trades.** Cooper, thatcher, foundry, lathe, flange, gasket, sift,
  trowel, spindle, awl, jig. Concrete nouns from work nobody in the category
  talks about.
- **Adjacent-category vocabularies.** Force an unrelated field to answer the top
  rung: sailing, jazz, beekeeping, card games, lighthouses, diner back offices.
  The obvious answer lives in your category, so this is the fastest way to break
  obvious thinking.
- **Classical roots.** Greek and Latin morphemes, but as raw material, never as
  a naming scheme. `-a` and `-um` stems are the slop pattern, not a shortcut.
- **One collection emptied completely.** Placek's team built a database of "small
  things" drawn from tiny cars, microprocessors, seeds, and stitches, because the
  resonance lived in the collection, not in any one list. Build one for your
  product.

Full source lists: [references/word-sources.md](references/word-sources.md).

**Volume, and who does it.** Target 150 names per pass. Most people stall at 50
to 100; the professional bar is around 2,000 candidates for one name, which is
why quantity is a technique and not enthusiasm. Generate alone or in a pair, never
in a group: a group evaluates too early, and early evaluation is what kills a name
set. When somebody does react, react like this: "that's interesting" or "how could
we make that clear legally", never "that'll never pass".

**Three passes, briefed differently.** Pass one knows only the product. Pass two
knows the product plus one dimension (heat, quiet, weight, night). Pass three
knows an adjacent category instead of the product. Briefed separately, the three
produce three different word families; briefed together they collapse into one.

*Done when:* 150 or more candidates exist per pass, each tagged with the bucket it
came from, and the three passes are visibly different word families.

## 3. Stay in the approximate zone

Draw a line. At one end put bizarre, absurd, illegal ideas; at the other, safe and
workable ones; in the middle, **approximate thinking**. Only the middle is useful,
and it is where most people never go.

Practically: if nobody raises an eyebrow at the whole list, the list came from the
safe end. Keep the eyebrow-raisers. Lexicon's clients resisted Blackberry and it
became a five billion dollar brand; a name that polarises often has force inside
it, which is why Intel's Andy Grove liked `Pentium`.

*Done when:* at least a third of the shortlist makes somebody hesitate, and you
can name which ones and why.

## 4. Architect the sound

Say every candidate aloud. A name is heard before it is read, so sound carries
meaning the logo cannot.

- **Processing fluency.** Aim for two or three syllables, one obvious
  pronunciation, one obvious spelling. If you have to spell it to a friend, or a
  stranger reads it back wrong, it is already dead. `Codeium` failed this way:
  nobody could spell it, search it, or say it, and the company renamed to
  `Windsurf`.
- **Sound symbolism, with the receipts.** People given two made-up words, `mil`
  and `mal`, rate `mal` as the bigger object; given `takete` and `maluma`, they
  match takete to spiky shapes and maluma to round ones, and the effect holds
  across cultures. Bilabial consonants read as soft. So the sound is not
  decoration: front-load consonants that match the intended feel. Plosives (b, d,
  g, k, p, t) and hard c read decisive and energetic; sibilants (s, z, sh) read
  fast and sleek; liquids and nasals (l, m, n, r) read soft and warm. The first
  sound does its work in the first few hundred milliseconds, so put the strongest
  one there.
- **Cut the friction.** Keep q, x, and silent letters away from a name that ships
  internationally, and keep numerals and hyphens out of the middle. One exception
  worth knowing: `x` reads as new and technical when the product really is
  (`SpaceX`), and as noise when it is not.

*Done when:* every remaining candidate has one obvious reading, and any candidate
whose sound argues against the top rung is struck out.

## 5. Believability test

A name is judged in context and never alone, so mock the finalists into the real
world. For each one, write:

- a headline that would run in a news story about the product;
- a billboard: the name plus six words, nothing else;
- an app store line: name, one-line description, icon label;
- one spoken sentence from a customer using it naturally, such as "I put it in
  ___";
- the handle and domain: `name.app`, `name.com`, `@name`, and what actually came
  back;
- the verb test: "I'll ___ it" or "just ___ it". If the name cannot be a verb and
  the product gets used often, say so out loud, because verbability is what turns
  a name into a word.

Also run the slop audit in [references/anti-slop.md](references/anti-slop.md) over
everything still standing. It is ten structural checks, not taste.

Then cut to exactly three, because a shortlist of fifteen is a pile and three
forces a decision. Give each of the three one line of reasoning: the bucket it
came from, the top rung it hits, and the ban-list shape it avoids.

*Done when:* exactly three names remain, each proven in every context above, each
carrying its one-line reason, and each surviving the slop audit.

## 6. Verify before committing

The best name in the world is worthless if it is taken, so check now rather than
after you are attached.

For each finalist, in this order: search the exact name (an existing company,
product, or notable person); check the domain plus the relevant suffix and say
what you actually found rather than assuming it is free; search the USPTO
trademark database in the software or closest class; check the app stores and
GitHub for a collision; and check the word in two other languages you care about
for an unfortunate meaning. Record a verdict of live, taken, or close-but-usable,
with the evidence and where you found it. A name with a live company in the same
class is out, so reorder the shortlist instead of lowering the bar.

Say what the search showed and stop there. Do not describe any of this as legal
clearance.

*Done when:* every finalist carries a live or taken verdict with its source, and
the final two are drawn from the live ones.

## 7. Hand back the kill list

Put the three survivors in the table shape the assignment expects, then add the
names you killed and the pattern each one matched. Two survivors out of six is a
healthy ratio, and the kill list is what teaches the team to run the filter
without you next time. A shortlist with no kill list teaches nothing.

*Done when:* the deliverable carries the survivors with reasons and the rejected
names with the pattern each one failed.
