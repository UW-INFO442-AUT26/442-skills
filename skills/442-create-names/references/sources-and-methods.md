# Sources and methods

Where the rules in this skill come from, so a reviewer can check them and a
student can go deeper. Six people and documents, deliberately not one school of
thought, plus the two facts the advice rests on.

## David Placek, Lexicon Branding

Named Swiffer, Blackberry, Pentium, Febreze, Sonos, Impossible Burger, Vercel,
Windsurf. The main source for the process.

- **Not a great name, the right name.** "Nothing you do in your brand will be
  used more often or for longer than your name." A right name is original in
  context, processing fluent, and unexpected, and most people drop the third.
- **Category first.** On a fiber brief: if every competitor says "fiber", do not
  go there. Then move to the felt benefit ("you feel lighter") and hunt that
  territory instead.
- **Quantity as technique.** Clients manage 50 to 100 names; Lexicon generates
  around 2,000. Most are bad and that is fine.
- **Alone or in pairs.** Group brainstorming "creates peer pressure and
  evaluation too early". Three teams briefed differently produce three directions.
- **The approximate zone.** Between bizarre and safe sits approximate thinking,
  which is the only useful part of the line.
- **Do not evaluate early.** "That'll never pass legal" becomes "how could we
  modify it so it clears legal". Encouragement shares a root with courage.
- **Proof of concept.** Present the name inside believable usage, because most
  people cannot judge a bare word.
- **Swiffer.** P&G wanted `Pro Mop` and could not clear it. The team repositioned
  the product away from mopping, toward light and easy, and the name followed.
  `Ready Mop` was instantly understood and lost.
- Interviews: [My First Million, full
  transcript](https://www.lexiconbranding.com/naming-billion-dollar-companies-the-art-and-science-behind-great-brand-names/)
  and [Focus on Brand](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I2j1U5auw_g).

## Paul Graham

Founder-side, and useful precisely because he disagrees with the mystique.

- **Naming is a separate skill.** "You can be a great startup founder but
  hopeless at thinking of names." In office hours he found a good name in 20
  minutes 80 percent of the time.
- **The .com is a signal.** "A marginal domain suggests you're a marginal
  company." His data point: 100 percent of the top 20 YC companies by valuation
  own their `.com`, 94 percent of the top 50.
- **Almost any name works.** "Almost any word or word pair that is not an
  obviously bad name is a sufficiently good one", and there are more cheap
  domains than people believe.
- **Names take on the tint of the thing.** He hated `Reddit` and asked them to
  change it. He was wrong.
- Sources: [Change Your Name](https://paulgraham.com/name.html) (2015) and
  [Startup Names](http://aux.messymatters.com/pgnames.html) (2006).

The tension with Placek is real and worth telling a student: Placek sells
asymmetric advantage from a name, Graham argues the name is downstream of the
product. Both are right in their own stakes. For a ten week class project, Graham
is the closer fit, which is why this skill blocks the descriptive names and does
not promise a billion dollar brand.

## Anthony Shore, Operative Words

A linguist's view, and the reason this skill cares about product descriptors.

- 37 years, 275 names, trained in linguistics at UC Santa Cruz, 13 years running
  naming at Landor, a year at Lexicon. Named Qualcomm Snapdragon, Adobe
  Lightroom, Virgin Voyages.
- **"Six words or fewer."** Succinct brand expression as the whole discipline.
- **The descriptor defines the product.** A truly new thing needs a new category
  descriptor, not a new adjective. Students building something novel should name
  the category too, because that is what makes the name mean something.
- **Global and native-speaker checks** belong in the process, not after it.
- [operativewords.com](https://operativewords.com/about).

## Alexandra Watkins, Eat My Words

The most usable filter, and the one a student can run in five minutes.

- **SMILE:** suggestive, memorable, imagery, legs, emotional.
- **SCRATCH:** spelling-challenged, copycat, restrictive, annoying, tame, curse of
  knowledge, hard-to-pronounce.
- [SMLE and SCRATCH test](https://eatmywords.com/smile-scratch-test/).

## Trademark law, the Abercrombie spectrum

Not a style opinion, a ranking with consequences. From
[Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trademark_distinctiveness)
and the general US spectrum:

| Strength | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest | Fanciful | Kodak |
| Strong | Arbitrary | Apple, for computers |
| Middle | Suggestive | Airbus, Blu-ray |
| Weak | Descriptive | Salty, for saltines (needs acquired meaning) |
| Unprotectable | Generic | escalator, trampoline |

Students reach for descriptive because it feels safe. It is the least
protectable and the most crowded, which is why the skill aims one rung up.

## Sound symbolism, the science

The claim that some sounds carry size, sharpness, or softness is testable, and it
has been tested.

- **Magnitude symbolism.** Sapir (1929): given `mil` and `mal`, most people rate
  `mal` as the larger object. High front vowels read small, low and back vowels
  read big.
- **Takete and maluma.** Köhler (1929): people match `takete` to spiky shapes and
  `maluma` to round ones.
- **Bouba and kiki.** Ramachandran and Hubbard (2001) renamed the effect, and it
  has been reproduced across cultures and languages.
- **Tactile symbolism.** Bilabial consonants read as soft, plausibly because lips
  are soft.
- Plosives and hard c read decisive, sibilants read fast, liquids and nasals read
  warm. Front-load the sound that matches the promise. A name whose sound argues
  with its promise loses every time it is spoken.
- Background: [sound symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_symbolism).

## Two facts to keep straight

1. **Nobody can promise a name will make a product succeed.** Placek can show
   names that helped; Graham can show a name he wanted changed that became
   permanent. Say which claim you are making.
2. **Generated text has a dialect.** Any model asked for names without a filter
   returns morphological assembly, because that is the highest-probability
   pattern in its training data. This is why the audit exists and why it is
   structural rather than aesthetic.
