# The anatomy of a persona that works

A persona earns its place when a reader can predict what this person does
next, and can be wrong about it. Every field below exists to make a
prediction possible.

Write the fields in this order. Each one narrows the next.

## 1. Name and one-line archetype

A name people can say out loud, and a phrase that carries the point.

- Weak: `Sarah, 34, marketing manager, tech-savvy`
- Strong: `Sarah Okafor, 34, keeps the spreadsheet everyone else trusts`

The archetype is the job the persona does in the cast. "Keeps the
spreadsheet everyone trusts" predicts behavior: she will not adopt a tool
that makes her the last to know.

## 2. The situation, not the demographic

Age, city, and job title are cheap. What matters is the constraint the
person is under.

- Weak: `Works at a mid-size company.`
- Strong: `Runs the Tuesday stock count on a clipboard, then retypes it
  into a shared sheet at 6pm because the handheld scanner only talks to
  the old system.`

A reader can now picture the workaround, and the workaround is the thing
your product has to beat.

## 3. Goal and hidden goal

The stated goal is what they would tell you. The hidden goal is what they
would not.

- Stated: `Get the count done before the store opens.`
- Hidden: `Not be the person who got the count wrong in front of the
  district manager.`

Hidden goals explain resistance that looks irrational. A tool that is
faster but less auditable fails the hidden goal, and she will not use it.

## 4. The friction, named three ways

- Functional: what does not work (`the scanner and the sheet disagree`).
- Emotional: what it costs them (`Sunday night dread`).
- Social: who sees the failure (`the district manager, monthly`).

One persona can have all three. A persona with only a functional friction
is a bug report, not a person.

## 5. What they do today instead

The workaround, named concretely, with the tools in it. This is the
strongest single line in a persona, because it is evidence the pain is
real and it sets the bar your product must clear.

- Weak: `Uses spreadsheets.`
- Strong: `Texts photos of the clipboard to herself, then types them up
  with the radio on, so a mistake costs an hour to find.`

## 6. How they decide

Who they ask, what makes them stop, what makes them abandon. Predictions
about adoption come from here, not from the pain.

- `Will not try anything a coworker has not already used for a month.`
- `Abandons any tool that asks for a login before showing value.`

## 7. A quote in their vocabulary

One line, in their words, not the product's.

- Weak: `I need a more efficient inventory solution.`
- Strong: `If it makes me re-count, I'd rather keep the clipboard.`

## 8. The decisions this persona should shape

One to three, written as decisions, not traits.

- Weak: `Cares about accuracy.`
- Strong: `Put the discrepancy list on the same screen as the count, so
  she never has to hold a number in her head.`

If a persona cannot produce a decision, it is not a persona. Cut it or
merge it.

## 9. The evidence label

Mark how each load-bearing detail was learned:

- `told` from a real conversation
- `read` from a survey, a review, an article, or the course material
- `assumed` from pattern matching

One label per detail that a design decision rests on. This is the honest
part, and it is the part a reviewer will check. A cast that is 80 percent
`assumed` is fine as a hypothesis. It is not fine when it is presented as
research.

## Length

Half a page per persona. Long enough to predict, short enough to hold in
your head while arguing about a feature. A persona that needs a page and a
half is two personas.
