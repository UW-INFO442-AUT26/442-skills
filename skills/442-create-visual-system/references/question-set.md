# Question set

Seven questions, one per message, each with the recommendation you carry into it.
Skip any question you can answer from the code. Ask about reactions, never about
adjectives.

## 1. Who is looking at it, and what are they doing?

The one that sets the register. A shift supervisor checking a list at 6am, a
student comparing three options, and a caseworker filing a claim want different
interfaces, and none of them wants to be delighted.

Recommendation to offer: from the audience and the moment of use, name a register
in one word and say why. `Utilitarian` for a tool used under time pressure,
`institutional` for something that has to look accountable, `friendly` for a
consumer product people return to, `craft` for anything whose value is the making.

## 2. Show me an interface you find easy to look at, and one you find hard.

The highest-value question in the set, and the one students find easiest.
Reactions are reliable where adjectives are not. Ask for products they already
use, not design references from a gallery.

Recommendation to offer: name the two or three properties you can actually
extract from their example (density, type weight, amount of color, how much
chrome), and check them back. "You picked a tool with almost no borders and one
green accent. That is what you are reacting to."

## 3. How much does one screen have to hold?

Density decides the spacing ladder, the type scale, and whether a card is a
container or a table row. An app that shows one number per screen and an app that
shows forty rows are different visual languages, not different skins.

Recommendation to offer: from the busiest screen in the inventory, pick
`comfortable` (few items, one idea per block, generous space) or `compact` (many
items, scan-first, tighter rhythm) and name which screen forced the choice.

## 4. What should the type do?

Two decisions, not one: the class of typeface and the job it does. A grotesque
reads neutral and technical, a humanist sans reads approachable, a serif reads
institutional or editorial, a mono reads exact.

Recommendation to offer: from the register, name one class and one weight
strategy ("one family, two weights, no italics" is the safest default), and say
what it would cost to change later.

## 5. What is color for here?

Color needs a job, or it becomes decoration. The jobs that survive are: mark one
primary action, show state, carry identity, or separate regions. Pick at most two.

Recommendation to offer: one accent with one job, plus a neutral scale, and a
status set that is only used for status. Warn about the specific failure: three
accents means none of them means anything.

## 6. Corners, borders, or shadows?

This one question sets the whole geometry of the language and students have
opinions about it immediately. Sharp and bordered reads precise and institutional.
Rounded and shadowed reads soft and consumer. Flat with hairlines reads editorial
or technical.

Recommendation to offer: one of the three, tied to the register from question 1,
with a radius scale to go with it. Then say the rule that keeps it consistent:
pick one and use it everywhere, because a rounded card next to a square one looks
like a mistake rather than a choice.

## 7. What is the one thing that must feel like us?

The signature move: the thing a classmate would recognize in a screenshot. It can
be a numbered index, a hairline rule, a monospace label, a colored left edge, an
oversized numeral, a dotted grid. One thing, applied consistently.

Recommendation to offer: propose one signature move drawn from the product's
domain or from the register, and name the screen where it will be most visible.

## The verbal four

Copy is where most projects sound identical, so ask these even when the team came in
asking about colors. One question per message, same shape.

### 8. Does it say "you" or "the user"?

Recommendation to offer: second person, everywhere, including errors. Name the
reason: an interface that says "the user must enter a date" is a form talking about
someone who is not in the room.

### 9. How much does it explain?

Brevity is a product decision. A tool used daily by the same people can say
`Saved.` A tool used twice a year should say what it saved and where it went.
Recommendation to offer: one position on the explanatory-to-compressed dial, tied to
how often the audience sees the screen.

### 10. Does it ever apologize, and does it ever joke?

Two separate decisions and both worth asking, because the answers are usually
different. Recommendation to offer: apologize by stating what happened and what to
do, never by emoting; allow humor only where nothing is at stake, and never in an
error.

### 11. What does it refuse to say?

The most useful one. Ask for three words the product would never use. Teams have
strong, correct instincts here ("we would never say 'leverage'") and it gives the
banned list in `voice.md` real ownership rather than an imported word list.

## What to write down after each answer

Keep a running list in the conversation. Each line is the decision, the choice,
and the student's reason:

```text
Register: utilitarian, because the supervisor reads it at 6am, one-handed.
Density: compact, because the rota screen shows 14 rows.
Color: one accent for the primary action only, status colors for state only.
Geometry: borders and small radius, because it reads precise rather than soft.
Signature move: the time, set in mono at the left of every row.
Voice: second person, contractions, no exclamation marks.
Brevity: one sentence, because the screen is seen daily.
Refuses: leverage, empower, "synergy", "Oops".
```

That list is the raw material for the three directions, and it is what `design.md`
gets written from. If a line has no reason, it is not a decision yet.
