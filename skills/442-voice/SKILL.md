# Edit for voice

Make prose read like a person wrote it. Every fact, idea, and opinion in the
draft stays. What changes is the expression: the wording, the structure, and the
rhythm.

This is not a filter that swaps out words a model is known to like. Strip the
tell-words and you get a second flavour of the same problem. The patterns under
the words are what you hunt, and the full catalogue, with examples, is in
[references/patterns.md](references/patterns.md). Read it before step 2.

Two words to think with:

- **Slop** is prose that sounds like something while saying nothing. That is what
  you are hunting.
- **Voice** is the writer's own judgement showing through: a specific opinion, a
  real number, a sentence only this person would write. That is the target.

No word list separates them, because the difference is whether anything is behind
the sentence. A three-item list is slop when it decorates and voice when the three
items are true.

## 1. Read for the argument before you change a line

Read the whole draft first, then answer for yourself:

- What is this actually saying?
- Which one to three ideas carry it?
- Where does the writer's own judgement, doubt, or experience show up?

That last answer is your raw material. Anything that only restates, softens, or
decorates one of those ideas is a candidate for deletion.

**Done when** you can state the argument back in two sentences and name the lines
that carry it.

## 2. Cut the slop

Work through [references/patterns.md](references/patterns.md) against the draft.
The expensive ones:

- **Significance announced instead of shown.** "Something shifted." "This raises
  an important question." If the idea matters, say the idea.
- **Decorative symmetry.** Three-item lists, mirrored clauses, and contrast frames
  ("not just X, but Y") built for cadence rather than because the contrast is
  real.
- **Meaning repeated.** A sentence that paraphrases the one above it, or a closing
  line that re-says its own paragraph.
- **Scaffolding.** "Furthermore", "That said", "Ultimately", and the paragraph
  that announces what the next paragraph will do. Most paragraphs can just begin.
- **Neat resolution.** The last paragraph that summarizes the piece and gestures
  at the future. End where the thought ends.

**Done when** a deletion takes a sentence and leaves no fact behind. That is the
test: if nothing informational leaves, the sentence was significance, filler, or
scaffolding.

## 3. Give it a rhythm

Slop runs on one tempo: every sentence the same length, every paragraph the same
shape, every section closing on a line that lands.

- Put a short sentence beside a long one.
- Let a paragraph end flat instead of on a punchline.
- Let one section run longer than another, because it has more to say.
- Keep the writer's punctuation and their odd phrasing when it is theirs.

**Done when** you can read it aloud without hearing a metronome, and no two
neighbouring paragraphs have the same shape.

## 4. Make it concrete with what is already there

Vague prose usually has the detail sitting a line away, unsaid. Pull it up: name
the file, the number, the command, the day, the thing that actually happened.

Take "I opened the invoice and saw the total was wrong" over "this experience
highlighted an important realization".

**Never invent what is not in the draft.** No manufactured anecdotes, statistics,
quotes, dates, or opinions. Concrete means using the detail already on the page,
or asking the writer for it.

**Done when** every concrete noun in the draft is a real thing from the source,
and nothing has appeared that the writer did not say.

## 5. Rewrite the ending, then read the whole thing back

The last paragraph is where slop concentrates, because a model wants to close the
loop. Cut the lesson, the summary, and the look ahead, then end on the last real
observation, an open question, or a flat statement of what happens next.

Then read the draft back and ask of each paragraph: could this have appeared in
ten thousand other documents? Is it saying something, or sounding like something?

**Done when** the ending adds nothing after the thought has finished, and every
paragraph has survived that question.

## What's next

Read the piece out loud, or have it read to you. The sentences you stumble over
are the ones still carrying slop.
