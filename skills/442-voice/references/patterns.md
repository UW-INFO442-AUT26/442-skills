# The patterns, with examples

The catalogue behind `442-voice`. Each entry says what the pattern looks like and
what to do instead. None of it is a ban on a word or a punctuation mark: the
problem is repetition that decorates and says nothing, and the cure is a sentence
with something behind it.

## 1. Announcing significance

The text tells you an idea matters instead of showing you why. Delete the
announcement and state the idea.

| Slop | Instead |
|---|---|
| "Something shifted." | Say what changed. |
| "Everything changed after that." | Say what changed, and when. |
| "But here's the thing." | Cut it and start the sentence. |
| "This raises an important question." | Ask the question. |
| "At its core, ..." | Cut to the core. |
| "Perhaps the most important part is ..." | Name the part and let it stand. |
| "The implications are profound." | State one implication. |

The test: delete the sentence. If no fact leaves with it, it was significance.

## 2. Decorative symmetry

Models produce structure because structure reads as competence. Cut the
structure when the content does not have it.

- **Three-item lists** where two items would do, or where the third exists to
  complete the shape.
- **Mirrored clauses.** "It's not about the tool. It's about the thinking."
- **Contrast frames.** "Not just X, but Y." "X isn't about X. It's about Y."
  Use one when the contrast is real and you would have written it as a contrast
  anyway. Otherwise pick the half you mean.
- **Anaphora.** "It's the ability to plan. It's the ability to ship. It's the
  ability to decide."
- **Rule of three** applied to everything: three reasons, three steps, three
  examples, because three is the shape people expect.

Human writing is uneven. One reason is fine. Four is fine. The count should come
from the material.

## 3. Metaphor that carries nothing

Cut the metaphor that could have appeared in any document on any topic: the
journey, the double-edged sword, the slippery slope, the tip of the iceberg, the
tip of the spear, the tapestry, the mirror, the catalyst, the ecosystem, the
layers of an onion.

Do not replace it with a different generic metaphor. If the metaphor is doing real
work, make it specific to this subject, the way "the query is doing a table scan
on every keystroke" is specific and "the query is the engine of the app" is not.

## 4. Filler that reads as content

Sentences that report on the piece instead of adding to it:

- "This is important."
- "This is more complicated than it looks."
- "There are many factors to consider."
- "Everyone has their own perspective."
- "The world is changing fast."
- "Let's dive in."
- "In today's organizations, ..."

Also cut the padding sentence that exists to make a thin section feel solid. A
short section is allowed to be short.

## 5. Stacked qualification

Models hedge to stay safe, and hedging spreads: "arguably", "perhaps", "in many
ways", "to some extent", "it could be said", "generally speaking", "in a sense".

Keep uncertainty that the writer actually has, and say what it is ("I have not run
this on Windows"). Cut the hedging that only makes the sentence sound careful.
One qualifier per claim is usually one too many.

## 6. Transition scaffolding

Most paragraphs can start with their own first sentence. Cut:

- "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally", "In addition".
- "However", "That said", "On the other hand", "Nevertheless".
- "Meanwhile", "Ultimately", "In conclusion", "With that in mind".
- "This brings us to ...", "Now let's look at ...", "Next, we'll cover ...".

Keep a connective when the logic genuinely turns on it, which is rare. If two
paragraphs are related, the reader usually sees it without being told.

## 7. The template shape

Watch for a piece that has been poured into this mould, whether or not it fits:

hook, context, the problem, three reasons, a counterargument, the solution, the
takeaway, a closing line about the future.

Real writing follows the shape of the thinking. If the argument has four parts and
part two is longer, write that. If there is no counterargument, do not invent one
to fill the slot.

## 8. The summary ending

The final paragraph is the highest-risk part of the whole piece, because the model
wants to close the loop:

- "Ultimately, the future belongs to those who ..."
- "Perhaps the real lesson is ..."
- "The takeaway is simple ..."
- "As we move into a changing world, ..."

End where the thinking ends. An ending can be a specific observation, an
unresolved question, a sharp opinion, a small detail, or one flat sentence. It
does not have to summarize anything.

## 9. What not to overcorrect

The patterns above are not a list of banned features. Overcorrecting produces a
third kind of slop, so:

- **Do not ban punctuation or grammar on principle.** Em dashes, semicolons,
  colons, contractions, sentence fragments, and rhetorical questions all appear
  in good human writing. A repetition is the problem, not the mark.
- **Do not flatten a real voice.** If the writer is dry, keep dry. Blunt, keep
  blunt. Do not make them sound more professional, more confident, or more
  inspirational than the source.
- **Do not invent anything to sound concrete.** No made-up anecdotes, numbers,
  quotes, or experiences. Specificity comes from the source or from asking.
- **Do not deform into bullet points** because lists are easier to write. Some
  arguments are prose.
- **Do not add decoration**: extra headings, emoji, bold "key takeaways", a TL;DR,
  a summary box.
- **Do not optimize for passing an AI detector.** Detectors are unreliable, and
  the target is authorship, not a score.

**House style still applies.** This repo bans the em dash in its own files, and
likes short sentences. Those are this repo's choices, not rules about good
writing. Keep them out of the way when you edit somebody else's words.

## 10. The read-back test

Before you hand the draft over, interrogate each paragraph:

- Could this sentence have appeared in ten thousand other documents?
- Is it saying something, or sounding like something?
- Is it specific enough to be checked?
- Does it earn its place?
- Is this transition needed?
- Have I made the paragraph too symmetrical?
- Am I explaining what the reader can already infer?
- Am I trying to sound profound?
- Does the rhythm run on one tempo?
- Would a person actually say it this way?
- Is there a real judgement in here, or only competent wording?

A sentence that is polished and empty gets rewritten. A sentence that is slightly
unusual and unmistakably the writer's gets left alone.
